Children… Seafood Gumbo (Alfre’s Way)

Chef says: Now children, gumbo ain’t a soup and it ain’t a stew — it’s a life lesson in a pot. Shellfish, smoked bird, and okra holding hands. Serve it over rice like you mean it.

This gumbo came off Julius’s kitchen scans — the kind of pot that gathers a whole house. Alfre’s way means smoked turkey, sausage, okra, and the Gulf showing up late with shrimp and crab. You don’t rush gumbo. You let it teach you patience one ladle at a time.

Serves 6 to 8

Ingredients

  • 6 quarts spring water
  • 6 skinless, boneless chicken breast halves, cut into 2-inch pieces
  • 1 smoked turkey leg
  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 4 onions, diced
  • 5 green bell peppers, diced
  • 2 cloves garlic, minced
  • 2 teaspoons dried thyme leaves, crumbled
  • 1 link smoked turkey sausage, cut into 1/2-inch pieces
  • 1 16-ounce bag frozen cut okra
  • 1 tablespoon fresh lemon juice
  • 2 pounds shrimp, peeled and deveined
  • 3 blue crabs
  • 1/3 pound lump crabmeat
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 1 tablespoon Creole seasoning
  • Cayenne pepper to taste

How you do it

  1. In an 8-quart pot, bring water to boiling. Add the chicken and turkey leg and reduce heat to simmer.
  2. In a large skillet, heat the oil over medium heat until hot but not smoking and sauté the onions until browned, about 15 minutes. Add the green peppers, garlic and thyme. Cook, stirring occasionally, until the vegetables have softened, about 10 minutes, and add to the chicken mixture.
  3. Wipe out the skillet with a paper towel and heat the pan over medium heat. Add the sausage and sauté until browned. Add the okra and cook until thawed, about 5 minutes. Add the lemon juice (to cut the okra’s attitude) and mix well.
  4. Transfer the sausage mixture to the pot with the chicken and simmer, uncovered, over medium heat until chicken starts to shred and the gumbo thickens, about 1½ hours.
  5. Add the shrimp, crabs, crabmeat, and bay leaves. Cover and cook 10 minutes.
  6. Add Creole seasoning and cayenne. Taste it. Respect it. Serve over rice.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Brown the onions properly — pale onions make pale gumbo. Take the full 15 minutes.
  • Lemon juice on okra isn’t folklore; it softens the ‘slime’ so the pot stays silky, not sticky.
  • Seafood goes in last. Overcooked shrimp turn into pencil erasers — 8 to 10 minutes is plenty.
  • Taste cayenne at the table, not just the stove. Heat blooms as the pot sits.
  • Day-two gumbo is wiser. Cool uncovered briefly, then refrigerate; reheat gently.

Everyday Love Quick Cornbread

Chef says: Children, a man who can’t throw down cornbread on short notice is a man unprepared for life. Keep the mix ready. Keep the buttermilk honest.

Weeknight cornbread — the kind you bake while the greens simmer and somebody asks what’s for supper. Self-rising meal meets Jiffy, buttermilk keeps it tender, and a hot greased pan gives you that golden edge Southerners argue about in church parking lots.

Serves one happy pan

Ingredients

  • 2 cups self-rising cornmeal
  • 1 cup Jiffy cornbread mix
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1/3 cup vegetable oil

How you do it

  1. Preheat oven to 350°F. Grease a 13×9-inch baking pan with cooking spray and heat it in the oven while you make the batter.
  2. In a medium bowl, combine cornmeal and Jiffy mix. Add eggs, buttermilk, and oil; mix until moistened, adding a splash of water if needed.
  3. Spread in the hot pan and bake until golden, 15 to 20 minutes.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Hot pan first — cold batter hitting hot fat is how you get that crisp bottom crust.
  • Stop mixing the second it’s moistened. Overworked cornbread turns dense and sad.
  • Buttermilk should be room-temp-ish; ice-cold dairy shocks the rise.
  • If the batter looks like thick cake batter, splash in water until it pours — it should ribbon, not clump.

North Memphis Hoecakes

Chef says: These little cornmeal cakes got history in ’em, children. Skillet-fried, golden, and ready for molasses or gravy — don’t overthink it.

Hoecakes remember when cornmeal met a skillet and supper still needed to stretch. In North Memphis kitchens they stay thin, crisp-edged, and honest — molasses for sweet tooths, gravy for the serious, or plain beside beans.

Serves 4 to 6

Ingredients

  • 2 cups plain cornmeal
  • 1 cup Jiffy cornbread mix
  • 1 egg
  • Water (enough for a thin, smooth batter)
  • 1/4 cup canola or vegetable oil

How you do it

  1. Combine cornmeal, Jiffy mix, and egg; stir in water until the batter is thin and smooth.
  2. Heat oil to cover the bottom of a heavy skillet. Drop about 1/3 cup batter per cake. Cook until golden; flip and brown the other side.
  3. Drain on paper towels. Eat warm.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Batter should be thinner than pancake batter — think heavy cream, not muffin dough.
  • Oil depth matters: a skim isn’t enough; you want a shallow fry, not a dry pan.
  • Don’t crowd the skillet. Crowded cakes steam and turn pale.
  • Flip once. Peeking and poking breaks the crust you’re building.

Southern-Style Baked Grits

Chef says: Grits are comfort with a diploma. Breakfast, dinner, soak up the juices — when they’re good they’re great, and when they’re ordinary they’re still home.

Baked grits take the humble pot of morning comfort and dress it for supper — garlic, cheese that melts into the grain, eggs to set the casserole, cheddar on top like a gold crown. Church-supper energy in a 2-quart dish.

Serves 4 to 6

Ingredients

  • 4 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup grits
  • 1 tablespoon butter
  • 1 clove garlic, minced
  • 6 ounces bacon-flavored cheese
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 2 eggs, beaten
  • Pinch of paprika
  • 1/2 cup grated cheddar cheese

How you do it

  1. Preheat oven to 350°F. Butter a 2-quart baking dish.
  2. Boil water with salt; stir in grits and simmer until tender, about 15 minutes.
  3. Melt butter; sauté garlic about 1 minute. Add bacon-flavored cheese until melted; stir in milk.
  4. Fold garlic-cheese mixture into grits with eggs and paprika. Spread in dish; bake until set, about 40 minutes.
  5. Top with cheddar; bake until melted, about 15 minutes more.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Whisk grits in vigorously when they hit boiling water — lumps form in the first 30 seconds.
  • Temper the eggs: stir a spoon of hot grits into the beaten eggs before folding, or you’ll scramble them.
  • Let the casserole rest 10 minutes after baking so it slices clean instead of collapsing.
  • Bacon-flavored cheese can be sharp cheddar + a pinch of smoked paprika if your store doesn’t stock it.

“Hot Buttered” Fried Creamed Corn

Chef says: Children, when bacon fat meets sweet corn, time travel is real. Mama’s wood stove taught me that — your skillet can too.

This is summer cut off the cob and fried in bacon drippings until the sugars caramelize just a little, then wrapped in evaporated milk and butter. Not canned creamed corn’s cousin — its wiser older sibling.

Serves 4 to 6

Ingredients

  • 6 ears corn
  • 3 slices bacon
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 1 tablespoon all-purpose flour
  • 1 cup evaporated milk, warmed
  • Salt to taste
  • Pepper to taste

How you do it

  1. Cut kernels off the cobs. Fry bacon until crisp; drain and crumble.
  2. In the drippings, sauté corn until lightly browned. Add butter; stir in flour until smooth. Gradually add evaporated milk. Stir in bacon; cover and cook until thickened, about 10 minutes. Season with salt and pepper.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • After cutting kernels, scrape the cob with the back of the knife — that ‘milk’ is free flavor.
  • Warm the evaporated milk before adding so the flour doesn’t seize into lumps.
  • Brown the corn lightly before the milk goes in — that Maillard kiss is the whole point.
  • Cover for the last simmer so it doesn’t scorch; stir once or twice to check the bottom.

Cheddar Baked Beans

Chef says: Baked beans are America’s road trip in a dish — sweet, tangy, mysterious. I use the can when life is loud; from-scratch when love has time.

A pantry jazz improvisation: canned beans upgraded with peppers, a whisper of five-spice and curry, honey for shine, and cheddar cubes that melt into molten pockets. Weird spice list? That’s how family recipes get remembered.

Serves 4 to 6

Ingredients

  • 1 28-ounce can baked beans
  • 1/2 cup chopped onion
  • 1/2 cup chopped red bell pepper
  • 1/2 cup chopped green bell pepper
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons minced fresh cilantro
  • 1/4 teaspoon Chinese five-spice powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon dried basil
  • 1/8 teaspoon curry powder
  • 1/8 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • Pinch of ground black pepper
  • 1/4 to 1/3 cup honey or maple syrup
  • 1 tablespoon butter
  • 1 cup cubed sharp cheddar cheese

How you do it

  1. Preheat oven to 350°F.
  2. Place beans in a 1½-quart baking dish. Stir in onion, peppers, seasonings, and honey or maple syrup. Dot with butter; top with cheddar cubes.
  3. Bake 30 minutes, until cheese is bubbly and the spices announce themselves.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Cube the cheddar (don’t shred) so you get lava pockets instead of a sealed cheese lid.
  • Taste before baking — canned beans vary wildly in sweetness; adjust honey accordingly.
  • The tiny spice amounts matter: toast them briefly in your palm smell-test before dumping in.
  • Rest 5 minutes out of the oven so the sauce thickens instead of running all over the plate.

Homemade Baked Beans with Love

Chef says: Love ain’t on the ingredient list, children — you gotta bring that yourself. Navy beans, tomato paste, a little sweetness… and ribs on the side if you got ’em.

From-scratch navy beans when you want the quiet version — tomato paste stretched with spring water, brown sugar or honey, butter for roundness. Simple enough to cook while the ribs finish. Love is the part you can’t measure.

Serves 2 to 4

Ingredients

  • 1 cup spring water
  • 1/2 cup tomato paste
  • 3 cups cooked navy beans
  • 2 teaspoons brown sugar or honey
  • 2 teaspoons butter
  • 1/2 teaspoon all-purpose flour

How you do it

  1. Preheat oven to 300°F.
  2. Mix water and tomato paste until smooth; cook over medium heat 20 minutes.
  3. In a 1½-quart baking dish, combine beans, sugar or honey, butter, and flour. Stir in the tomato mixture.
  4. Bake 15 to 20 minutes. Serve with ribs if the spirit moves you.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Cook the tomato-water mixture the full 20 minutes — raw paste tastes metallic until it mellows.
  • Use already-cooked, well-drained beans; wet beans dilute the sauce into soup.
  • Low oven (300°F) keeps sugar from scorching while flavors marry.
  • A pinch of salt and black pepper (not on the original card) wakes everything up — season to taste.

Spicy Stewed Tomatoes

Chef says: Don’t sleep on stewed tomatoes, children. Better than the can, patient on the stove, a little cayenne for truth-telling.

Ripe beefsteaks broken down with maple, cider vinegar, and garden herbs until they surrender. Side dish, biscuit topper, or the bright note next to fried fish — cayenne keeps them honest.

Serves 2 to 4

Ingredients

  • 4 ripe beefsteak tomatoes, sliced
  • 1 tablespoon maple syrup
  • 1 tablespoon apple cider vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon chopped fresh basil
  • 1 teaspoon chopped fresh sage
  • 1 teaspoon chopped fresh oregano
  • Sea salt to taste
  • Pinch of cayenne pepper
  • 1 tablespoon butter (optional)

How you do it

  1. Combine tomatoes, syrup, vinegar, herbs, salt, cayenne, and enough water to cover.
  2. Bring to a boil; reduce heat and simmer until tomatoes fall apart, about 20 minutes.
  3. Stir in butter if using; cover and simmer 5 minutes more.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Use truly ripe tomatoes — hard winter ones stew into sour sadness.
  • Vinegar + maple is the balance: taste after 15 minutes and nudge sweet or sharp.
  • Don’t pulverize with a blender; the broken slices are the texture you want.
  • Butter at the end rounds acidity the way cream does in tomato soup.

Smashing Mashed Potatoes

Chef says: One house rule, children: no lumps. Evaporated milk, a little Miracle Whip, butter — mash until your problems get creamy too.

These aren’t fancy Parisian purée — they’re Southern smashed potatoes with evaporated milk for body and a spoon of Miracle Whip for tang that makes people ask what you put in them. Tell them it’s love. Or don’t.

Serves 4 to 6

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds boiling potatoes, peeled
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • 1½ cups evaporated milk
  • 2 to 3 tablespoons Miracle Whip
  • Sea salt to taste

How you do it

  1. Cover potatoes with water; boil and simmer until fork-tender, about 30 minutes.
  2. Drain and mash until lump-free. Stir in butter, evaporated milk, and Miracle Whip. Season with sea salt.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Start potatoes in cold salted water so they cook evenly — hot water = raw cores.
  • Drain hard, then return the pot to low heat 30 seconds to steam off extra moisture before mashing.
  • Warm the evaporated milk; cold milk makes gluey potatoes.
  • Miracle Whip goes in last, off heat — heat can make mayonnaise-style dressings separate.

Buttermilk Fried Chicken

Chef says: Children, fried chicken is a covenant. Buttermilk for tenderness, seasoned flour for the jacket, hot oil for the sermon. Respect the rest time or eat regret.

Sunday table icon across Black and white Southern kitchens alike — the bird that gathers cousins, church hats, and extra napkins. This home version leans on a buttermilk soak and a double dredge for that shatter-crisp crust.

Southern Top Ten · Cast-iron & crackling crust

Serves 4 to 6

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken (3–4 lb), cut into 8 pieces (or equivalent parts)
  • 2 cups buttermilk
  • 1 tablespoon hot sauce
  • 2 teaspoons salt (for brine)
  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 2 teaspoons black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1 teaspoon onion powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • Peanut or vegetable oil for frying

How you do it

  1. Whisk buttermilk, hot sauce, and 2 tsp salt. Submerge chicken; refrigerate 4 hours or overnight.
  2. Mix flour with remaining seasonings in a wide bowl.
  3. Heat 1½ inches oil in a heavy skillet or Dutch oven to 325–350°F.
  4. Remove chicken from buttermilk, letting excess drip. Dredge thoroughly in flour; rest on a rack 10 minutes. Optional: dip back in buttermilk and dredge again for extra crunch.
  5. Fry in batches, dark meat first, turning once, until deep golden and 165°F inside (thighs ~14–18 min, breasts ~10–14). Drain on a wire rack, not paper towels alone.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Oil at 325°F when chicken goes in — it drops; you want to settle around 300–325°F while frying.
  • Rest dredged chicken 10 minutes so the crust hydrates and sticks instead of falling off.
  • Wire rack > paper towels: steam under the bird softens crust if it sits flat on towels.
  • Don’t skip salt in the buttermilk — that’s your brine, not just a soak.
  • Never crowd: overcrowding collapses oil temp and you get greasy pale chicken.

Buttermilk Biscuits & Sausage Gravy

Chef says: Biscuits are architecture, children. Cold butter, gentle hands, hot oven. Gravy is forgiveness poured on top — sage sausage and black pepper doing the talking.

Breakfast that became anytime food — soft buttermilk biscuits split open under peppery sausage gravy. This is the plate that says the day can wait five more minutes.

Southern Top Ten · Flaky, peppery, forgiven

Serves 4 to 6

Ingredients

  • BISCUITS: 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 6 tablespoons cold unsalted butter, cubed
  • 3/4 to 1 cup cold buttermilk
  • GRAVY: 1 lb pork breakfast sausage
  • 1/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2½ to 3 cups whole milk
  • 1 tsp black pepper (or more)
  • Salt to taste
  • Pinch of cayenne (optional)

How you do it

  1. Preheat oven to 450°F. Whisk flour, baking powder, soda, salt. Cut in butter until pea-sized. Stir in buttermilk just until dough comes together.
  2. Turn onto floured board; pat to ¾-inch. Cut with a floured cutter — no twisting. Bake 12–15 minutes until tall and golden.
  3. Meanwhile, brown sausage in a skillet, breaking it up. Sprinkle flour over fat; cook 1 minute. Whisk in milk gradually; simmer until thick. Season boldly with pepper and salt.
  4. Split warm biscuits; ladle gravy over like you mean it.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Butter and buttermilk ice-cold — warm fat melts and you lose flake.
  • Never twist the biscuit cutter; twisting seals edges so they won’t rise.
  • Gravy wants more black pepper than you think — taste after it thickens.
  • If gravy goes pasty, splash milk; if thin, simmer longer — don’t dump more raw flour.

Cast-Iron Cornbread

Chef says: Real cornbread hears the skillet scream when batter hits hot bacon fat. No sugar arguments at my table — make it how your people taught you, but get that crust.

The skillet staple — pot-likker’s best friend, chili’s roof, collards’ sidekick. Preheat the iron, shimmer the fat, pour, and listen for the sizzle that means crust is coming.

Southern Top Ten · Skillet hymn

Serves 6 to 8 (10-inch skillet)

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons bacon drippings or oil
  • 1½ cups fine yellow cornmeal
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon sugar (optional)
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1½ cups buttermilk
  • 4 tablespoons melted butter

How you do it

  1. Put drippings in a 10-inch cast-iron skillet; place in oven and heat to 425°F.
  2. Whisk dry ingredients. In another bowl, whisk eggs, buttermilk, melted butter. Combine just until moistened.
  3. Carefully swirl hot fat to coat skillet; pour batter in (it should sizzle). Bake 20–25 minutes until golden and a tester comes out clean.
  4. Rest 5 minutes; slice wedges. Serve with butter, honey, or pot likker.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • The preheat is non-negotiable — cold iron = pale bottom.
  • Bacon drippings beat neutral oil for flavor; save them in a jar like treasure.
  • Pull when edges pull from the pan slightly; overbaking dries the crumb.
  • Sugar is regional theology — omit for savory Deep South style, keep a spoon for sweeter Mid-South tables.

Baked Macaroni & Cheese

Chef says: Children, mac and cheese is not a box and a prayer. It’s a béchamel with backbone, sharp cheddar with opinions, and a bake that bubbles at the edges like applause.

Soul-food and church-supper essential — creamy center, bronzed top, the dish that disappears first at the potluck. This version builds a real cheese sauce, then bakes it proud.

Southern Top Ten · Bubbling edges of joy

Serves 6 to 8

Ingredients

  • 1 lb elbow macaroni
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • 1/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 3 cups whole milk, warmed
  • 1 teaspoon dry mustard
  • 1/2 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • 3 cups shredded sharp cheddar
  • 1 cup shredded Gruyère or more cheddar
  • 1/2 cup crushed Ritz or buttered breadcrumbs (topping)
  • 2 tablespoons butter, melted (for topping)

How you do it

  1. Cook pasta 1–2 minutes shy of package directions; drain. Preheat oven to 350°F. Butter a 9×13 dish.
  2. Melt 4 Tbsp butter; whisk in flour 1–2 minutes. Gradually whisk in warm milk; simmer until thick. Season with mustard, paprika, salt, pepper.
  3. Off heat, stir in most of the cheeses until melted. Fold in pasta. Spread in dish; top with remaining cheese mixed with crumbs and melted butter.
  4. Bake 25–30 minutes until bubbling and golden. Rest 10 minutes before serving.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Undercook pasta — it finishes in the oven and stays al dente, not mush.
  • Warm milk into the roux prevents lumps; cold milk fights you.
  • Sharp cheddar for flavor, a little melty cheese (Gruyère/American) for stretch — all-cheddar can turn grainy if overheated.
  • Rest after baking: molten cheese needs a minute to set into scoopable glory.

Collard Greens with Smoked Turkey

Chef says: Greens ain’t boiled punishment, children — they’re a slow conversation with smoked meat and pot likker. Save that liquor. That’s liquid gold for cornbread dunking.

Year-round comfort and New Year’s luck — collards simmered with smoked turkey until silky, vinegar on the side for the bright folks, hot sauce for the brave. The pot likker is half the reason you cook them.

Southern Top Ten · Pot likker included

Serves 6 to 8

Ingredients

  • 2 bunches collard greens (about 2 lb), stemmed and chopped
  • 1 smoked turkey wing or leg
  • 1 large onion, sliced
  • 3 cloves garlic, smashed
  • 6 cups chicken stock or water
  • 1 tablespoon apple cider vinegar (plus more to serve)
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon red pepper flakes
  • Salt and black pepper to taste
  • Hot sauce for the table

How you do it

  1. Rinse greens thoroughly in several changes of water — grit hides in the folds.
  2. In a large pot, simmer smoked turkey, onion, garlic, and stock 30 minutes to start a broth.
  3. Add greens in handfuls as they wilt. Stir in vinegar, sugar, pepper flakes. Simmer uncovered or partly covered 45–90 minutes until tender to your liking.
  4. Season with salt and pepper. Serve with pot likker, extra vinegar, and hot sauce.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Wash like you mean it — sandy greens ruin the whole pot.
  • Smoked turkey > ham hock if you want deep smoke without as much salt bomb; still taste before salting.
  • Acid at the end brightens; too much early can keep greens from softening.
  • Don’t throw away pot likker — cornbread was invented to chase it.

Shrimp & Cheese Grits

Chef says: Lowcountry sent shrimp and grits up the coast and into every brunch menu, children. Creamy cheese grits below, garlicky shrimp and gravy above — that’s balance.

From fish-camp breakfast to white-tablecloth supper, shrimp and cheese grits carry the Lowcountry into the wider South. Stone-ground if you can get them; patience either way.

Southern Top Ten · Lowcountry in a bowl

Serves 4

Ingredients

  • GRITS: 1 cup stone-ground grits
  • 4 cups water or stock
  • 1 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 1 cup sharp cheddar, shredded
  • Salt to taste
  • SHRIMP: 1 lb large shrimp, peeled
  • 4 slices bacon, chopped
  • 1 small onion, diced
  • 1 bell pepper, diced
  • 2 cloves garlic
  • 1/2 cup chicken stock
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon Cajun seasoning
  • 2 green onions, sliced
  • Salt & pepper

How you do it

  1. Cook grits in water/stock per package (stone-ground often 30–45 min), stirring. Finish with milk, butter, cheddar, salt. Keep warm.
  2. Cook bacon until crisp; remove. In drippings, sauté onion and pepper until soft; add garlic 30 seconds.
  3. Add shrimp and Cajun seasoning; cook until just pink. Deglaze with stock and lemon; simmer briefly. Return bacon; adjust salt.
  4. Spoon grits into bowls; top with shrimp, gravy, and green onions.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Stone-ground grits need low-and-slow and frequent stirring — rush them and they stay gritty.
  • Pull shrimp the second they curl into C-shapes; O-shapes are overcooked.
  • Cheese off heat into grits so it melts smooth instead of breaking.
  • A squeeze of lemon at the end cuts the richness — don’t skip acid.

BBQ Pulled Pork

Chef says: Barbecue is geography you can taste, children. This is a home oven/smoker-friendly pork shoulder with a vinegar-kissed sauce — pick your regional allegiance at the table.

Pulled pork is patience meat — pork shoulder rubbed, cooked low until it surrenders, then dressed in sauce that argues vinegar vs tomato depending on which state raised you. Serve on buns or with slaw.

Southern Top Ten · Low & slow surrender

Serves 8 to 10

Ingredients

  • 4–5 lb boneless pork shoulder (Boston butt)
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon paprika
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1 teaspoon onion powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • SAUCE: 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup ketchup
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon mustard
  • 1 teaspoon hot sauce
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon pepper

How you do it

  1. Mix rub; coat pork generously. Rest refrigerated at least 2 hours or overnight.
  2. Cook low: smoker at 250°F, or oven at 300°F in a roasting pan with a splash of apple cider in the bottom, covered after the first hour, until 195–205°F internal and probe-tender (often 5–7 hours).
  3. Rest 30–45 minutes tented. Shred with forks, discarding big fat globs.
  4. Whisk sauce ingredients; warm. Toss pork with enough sauce to gloss; serve rest on the side.

Secrets of the Kitchen

  • Temperature targets matter more than clock time — pull when probe slides in like warm butter (~200°F).
  • Rest before pulling or juices run all over the board instead of staying in the meat.
  • Sauce goes on after pulling so you control wet vs dry; never boil the meat in sauce for hours.
  • Vinegar-forward sauce wakes fatty pork — taste and add sugar only if your crowd needs it.

Serves 8

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup sugar
  • 1/3 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 4 large egg yolks
  • 3 cups whole milk
  • 2 teaspoons vanilla extract
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 1 box vanilla wafers (~11 oz)
  • 4–5 ripe bananas, sliced

How you do it

Ingredients
